About Me

Hello! I’m Aiden Johnson, a senior at Carleton College studying Computer Science, Digital Arts and Humanities, Statistics, and Art History. My work sits at the intersection of human-computer interaction, UX research, and creative technology, with a focus on making complex technology more intuitive, accessible, and human-centered.

Through research, internships, and independent projects, I’ve designed interactive tools, conducted user-centered research, and developed AI-assisted technologies. At the Mayo Clinic’s Knowledge and Evaluation Research Unit, I developed a privacy-conscious research application, AI tools for analyzing clinical conversations, and user-centered interfaces for researchers and clinicians.

My portfolio reflects that multidisciplinary approach through research, data visualization, web development, Figma prototyping, digital humanities, and creative design. Portfolio

The problem

Clinical research teams needed a reliable way to compare how multiple coders evaluated the same patient encounters. The existing tool lacked data sharing, collaboration, and meaningful inter-rater reliability analysis.

My role

I developed EnIAC from a small feature request into a full qualitative research platform, designing and building the React/Electron interface, collaboration workflows, agreement tools, and visual interaction.

Methods

UX design, React, Electron, statistical analysis, iterative debugging, information architecture, interaction design, and user-centered problem solving.

  1. Build the foundation

    I created the clinical coding application with independent export/import capabilities without disrupting researchers' existing process. I built around the frameworks they wanted for the app, not vice versa.

    Add image

  2. Make agreement meaningful

    I built and validated multiple calculated measures of agreement among independent qualitative reviews, including ICC, Cohen's kappa, Fleiss' kappa, weighted kappa, and percent agreement. These are incredibly useful in qualitative assessment.

    Add image

  3. Design for real teams

    As requirements grew, I added role-based sharing, researcher rosters, progress tracking, transparent agreement views, CSV export, and light/dark theming.

    Add image

What I learned

Building EnIAC showed me that good research software is more than functionality. In the beginning, I wanted to make something that could be used for any kind of qualititave observation. However, it became more clear that what Mayo Clinic's Knowledge and Evaluation Research unit wanted was an assessment tool that was directly personalized to the work that they were specifically doing. This meant that I had to dig deep and observe their work flow to optimize the performance of the tool. As a plus, now the tool can be used for both the specific assessment frameworks that they requested, and is adaptable to ANY qualitative assessment, clinical or non-clinical research. EnIAC grew into a full-fledged platform used for qualitative assessment and was recognized with an award at the Midwest Endocrinology Society Conference.

The problem

Software can unintentionally exclude users when designers overlook accessibility and social context. This research examined whether teaching critical pedagogy in undergraduate software design could help students recognize and address these issues and create more accessible design.

My role

As a student researcher, I led qualitative coding of student reflections, assignments, and interviews. I developed and refined coding frameworks, analyzed patterns in student work, and collaborated on the study's quantitative and qualitative findings.

Add image

Methods

Mixed-methods research using the CritCon Index, qualitative coding, affinity diagramming, inductive codebook development, interviews, and non-parametric statistical analysis.

  1. Integrating critical pedagogy

    Students identified the embedded values in technology, and the CIDER elicitation technique throughout an undergraduate Software Design course to reconsider how their software may provide differential impacts for some users.

  2. Analyzing student reflection

    I independently coded student work and interviews, then refined codebooks through multiple rounds of analysis and consensus-building with my research partner.

    Add image

  3. Connecting reflection to action

    We compared students' proposed improvements with their final software to measure whether critical reflection translated into concrete inclusive design decisions.

What I learned

Students showed significant growth in their confidence discussing ethical and social impacts of software, and they reported feeling more personal agency in their work. Their work also became more attentive to diverse users, with all seven teams implementing accessibility-related improvements. The study showed how research can connect students' attitudes about inclusive design with their actual design decisions.
